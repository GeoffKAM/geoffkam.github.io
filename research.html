Job Market Paper

More Investors, Not Bigger Deals: Venture Capital Policy When Founders Become Investors

Paper (PDF, latest version)Slides

Paper (PDF) — coming soon

Abstract

Should venture-capital policy fund more investor slots (capacity to back one startup) or bigger deals (more capital per startup)? In a calibrated model, risk-averse founders search for investors and bargain over equity. Per government dollar, more slots raise founders’ welfare more for two reasons. First, a publicly funded slot creates more meetings; some founders exit rich enough to become accredited investors, whose slots create more, and so on. Mainly through this recycling loop, one slot ends up as 2.11 slots. Second, most startups fail, so risk-averse founders value their venture at no more than a fixed dollar amount. A 5% increase in publicly funded slots raises founders’ certainty-equivalent welfare by 2.90% and yearly exit value by 1.28% (51.2% and 50.9% through the recycling loop); equal spending on the bigger-deal policy, by 0.40% and 0.55% (45.9% and 15.1%). Each yearly dollar on slots adds $1.48 to yearly exit value; on the latter, $0.629. Indexing the 1982 accreditation threshold to inflation would cut founder-investors by two-thirds and founders’ welfare by a third.

Who Matches with Whom: Sorting and the Speed of Top Wealth Inequality

Draft (PDF)

Paper (PDF) — coming soon

Abstract

Which founders are paired with which venture funds sets how fat the top tail of wealth is. In the frictionless benchmark, more accurate pairing fattens the tail at once, whereas after a doubling of the variance of talent growth the tail takes 23.2 years to close half its gap, in an illustrative economy. Because a founder’s wealth is proportional to her talent times a power of her fund’s size, the tail’s Pareto exponent is a closed-form, decreasing function of how accurately the market ranks founders when pairing them with funds. Venture-deal estimates put that accuracy around 1989 between 0.275 and 0.354, on a scale from random matching (zero) to perfect sorting (one); since only closed deals are observed, it may lie outside that range. With sorting in 1989 tied to these data, a ramp in sorting from 1980 carries the rise of the top 0.1% wealth share to 2001 and 2016 but only part of its early rise to 1989.
