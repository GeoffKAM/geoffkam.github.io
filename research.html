Job Market Paper

More Investors, Not Bigger Deals: Venture Capital Policy When Founders Become Investors

Draft (PDF)Slides

Paper (PDF) — coming soon

Abstract

Should governments that back venture capital fund more investors or bigger deals? In a calibrated model, risk-averse founders search for investors and bargain over equity, and successful founders become investors. More investors win, for two reasons. First, founders whose exits qualify them as accredited investors fund later startups, so each investment slot that policy adds (room for one more startup in a portfolio) becomes 2.11. Second, most startups fail, so a risk-averse founder values her stake at a small fraction of its expected payoff, however large the deal. A 5% increase in publicly funded slots raises founders’ welfare by 2.90%; equal spending on bigger deals raises it by 0.40%. Per dollar, more investors add $1.48 a year of exit value; bigger deals add $0.629. Indexing the accredited-investor wealth test to inflation would shrink the founder-investor pool by two-thirds and cut founders’ welfare by a third.

Who Matches with Whom: Sorting and the Speed of Top Wealth Inequality

Draft (PDF)

Paper (PDF) — coming soon

Abstract

Which founders are paired with which venture funds sets how fat the top tail of wealth is. In the frictionless benchmark, more accurate pairing fattens the tail at once, whereas after a doubling of the variance of talent growth the tail takes 23.2 years to close half its gap, in an illustrative economy. Because a founder’s wealth is proportional to her talent times a power of her fund’s size, the tail’s Pareto exponent is a closed-form, decreasing function of how accurately the market ranks founders when pairing them with funds. Venture-deal estimates put that accuracy around 1989 between 0.275 and 0.354, on a scale from random matching (zero) to perfect sorting (one); since only closed deals are observed, it may lie outside that range. With sorting in 1989 tied to these data, a ramp in sorting from 1980 carries the rise of the top 0.1% wealth share to 2001 and 2016 but only part of its early rise to 1989.
