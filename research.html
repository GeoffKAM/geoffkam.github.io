Job Market Paper

Financing (from) the Rich: Recycled Founder Wealth, Idiosyncratic Risk, and the Venture Capital Supply Multiplier

Draft (PDF)Slides

Paper (PDF) — coming soon

Abstract

Per public dollar, funding more venture financiers does more for founders than funding bigger deals. In a calibrated general-equilibrium search model, risk-averse founders bargain with financiers, and successful founders’ exit wealth becomes new financier supply. Two forces drive the gap. Because accredited wealth is scarce, recycling creates a supply multiplier: each financier slot that policy adds becomes 2.11. A risk ceiling caps what an undiversified founder gains from a bigger deal: because most ventures fail, she values a single venture at no more than about 11% of its mean claim in the calibration, however large the deal. A 5% step in seeded financier slots raises founder welfare by 2.90% and exit value by 1.28%; a step in capital per deal of the same public cost raises them by only 0.40% and 0.55%. Each public dollar on more financiers also creates $1.48 of exit value a year, against $0.629 for bigger deals. The accredited-investor threshold gates the recycled supply: indexing it would shrink the recycled pool by two-thirds in equilibrium and cut founder welfare by a third and exit value by 17.2%.

Who Matches with Whom: Sorting and the Speed of Top Wealth Inequality

Draft (PDF)

Paper (PDF) — coming soon

Abstract

Which founders are paired with which venture funds sets how fat the top tail of wealth is. In the frictionless benchmark, more accurate pairing fattens the tail at once, whereas after a doubling of the variance of talent growth the tail takes 23.2 years to close half its gap, in an illustrative economy. Because a founder’s wealth is proportional to her talent times a power of her fund’s size, the tail’s Pareto exponent is a closed-form, decreasing function of how accurately the market ranks founders when pairing them with funds. Venture-deal estimates put that accuracy around 1989 between 0.275 and 0.354, on a scale from random matching (zero) to perfect sorting (one); since only closed deals are observed, it may lie outside that range. With sorting in 1989 tied to these data, a ramp in sorting from 1980 carries the rise of the top 0.1% wealth share to 2001 and 2016 but only part of its early rise to 1989.
